# TABLE BASEBALL-LITTLE

## RULES OF TABLE BASEBALL-LITTLE

## I. CARDS

The game is conducted with cards held by the batter and other cards held by the fielder (the pitcher).

The batter has the following cards: High Swing, Low Swing, Flat Swing, Hit & Run, Walk (Base on Balls), Stolen Base, Sacrifice, Box.

The pitcher has the following cards: High Fastball, High Curve, High Slider, Low Fastball, Low Curve, Low Slider, Inside Fastball, Outside Curve, Inside Slider, No Pitch.

### NUMBER OF CARDS

**Classic format:** The batter has a total of 16 cards (3 High Swing, 3 Low Swing, 3 Flat Swing, 3 Hit & Run, 1 Walk, 1 Stolen Base, 1 Sacrifice, 1 Box). The pitcher has 12 cards (1 of each, except 3 No Pitch).

**Reduced format:** The batter has a total of 12 cards (2 High Swing, 2 Low Swing, 2 Flat Swing, 2 Hit & Run, 1 Walk, 1 Stolen Base, 1 Sacrifice, 1 Box). The pitcher has the same 12 cards (1 of each, except 3 No Pitch). In this reduced format, the player is forced to use more strategies.

### HOW ARE CARDS CHOSEN?

Cards are played by voluntary choice of the player. They are not thrown at random. Once chosen and played, the card is not picked back up — it is discarded.

At the start of every half-inning, both players' hands are replenished to their full set of cards (batter and pitcher decks). Cards are only "spent" within the current half-inning.

### WHAT IS THE PURPOSE OF THE CARDS?

The cards drive the game because through them I gain access to the play. There are batting cards, pitching cards, and strategy cards. With the batting cards the batter connects, but then must gain access to the play, and the fielding player places their fielders according to the type of batter (depending on whether they are right-handed or left-handed, a power hitter, or a contact hitter).

### INDIVIDUAL CHARACTERISTICS OF THE CARDS

**Batter's cards:** All swings are used to connect and reach the bases produced by the hit, or to be out. A High Swing is struck out by a low pitch, a Low Swing is struck out by a high pitch, a Flat Swing is struck out by an outside or inside pitch, and so on for the other cards. This is what the congruence table is for. Some cards have more complex individual characteristics, which we will list here:

1. **Hit & Run** — Functions like a swing if there are no runners on base, meaning the batter connects or strikes out. But if there are runners on base, they start running with the swing, and if the batter gets a hit, the runner(s) advance one extra base. If the batter strikes out and it is not the third out, the fielder must forcefully flick the corresponding arrow (found on the field) to try to throw out the runner. For example, if there are runners heading to second and third base, the fielder must choose where to throw — whether to try to get the runner at third or second out. Another very peculiar characteristic of this card is that it breaks up the double play: if the batter hits into a double play, only the batter is out, not the runner — as long as it is not a runner going from third to home; in that case, this peculiarity does not apply. The runner advances to home via hit, sacrifice, steal, etc.

2. **Walk (Base on Balls)** — This is a strategy card: the batter takes the base or strikes out. There is no access to hitting here.

3. **Sacrifice** — This is a card used to advance a runner from any base to the next, but the batter is always out. If the batter strikes out, there is no play, but if the sacrifice is successful, the batter is always out regardless. In the game, a sacrifice can also be carried out on the field: if there is a fly ball to the outfield, a "tag up" can be made to execute the sacrifice.

4. **Stolen Base** — The steal can be done in 2 ways: with a card and on the field. With the card, the player can attempt to have the runner(s) stealing be safe or be thrown out. If safe, all runners are safe. If it is, for example, a double or triple steal, all are safe; but if the runner is out, only the lead runner is out. It is a risky strategy — only with the Outside/Inside cards is the runner safe; with all other cards, the runner is out. With this card, a player can even steal home plate, and this is the only way to make this play in table baseball. The other type of steal takes place on the playing field itself, where there is an option to steal second, third, or both, and the pitcher is the one who flicks the arrow to try to make the out.

5. **Box** — This is a card to protect the batter: if the batter plays it and the pitcher chooses one of the No Pitch cards, then a base on balls is awarded. If the batter uses Box and the pitcher plays any other card, then "there is no play" — the game simply continues and each player "burns" those cards. Note: some players prefer to use the Box differently — that is, if Box meets No Pitch, instead of a walk, the runners already on base advance. The walk is only awarded if there are no runners on base (however, this option must be agreed upon by both players beforehand).

6. **No Pitch** — This is the pitcher's main defensive tool. The pitcher has 3 No Pitch cards and can play them freely because only if the batter plays Box is a walk awarded (as explained before). But if the batter chooses any of the swings, sacrifice, or walk cards, there is no play (the cards are "burned"). It is different if the batter plays Hit & Run. In that circumstance, the lead runner is thrown out and the same batter continues at bat. But if there are no runners on base, the Hit & Run functions like any swing — the cards are simply "burned." If the batter plays a Stolen Base card, the runner is caught off base, the lead runner is thrown out, and the same batter continues batting.

## II. PLAYING FIELD

The table baseball field faithfully reproduces a real baseball field.

It has a **DIRECTION** that goes from A (the left field line) to O (the right field line). The center is H (which runs through the entire center field).

It has a **DEPTH** that goes from number 1 (which corresponds to the pitcher's level) to number 12, which is a home run.

There is a hit-depth spinner and a hit-direction spinner. To determine where the ball lands, you flick (forcefully) an arrow.

**DEPTH spinner of the hit** — This comprises 3 circles in one, depending on the batter's characteristics: whether the batter is Light, Medium, or Heavy. A Light batter has a higher probability of hitting short, while a Heavy batter has a higher probability of a long hit.

**DIRECTION circle of the hit** — This comprises 2 circles in one. It depends on whether the batter is right-handed or left-handed. A right-handed batter is more likely to hit toward left field, and a left-handed batter is more likely to hit toward right field.

**Stolen Base on the Field** — There is a circle where flicking the arrow determines whether the runner is out at second or third. If there is only one runner, the fielder tries to throw out the runner at the base they are attempting to steal by flicking the arrow; but if it is a double steal, the pitcher must state which runner they want to try to throw out.

**Sacrifice Fly on the Field** — There is a circle with 3 options, depending on the depth of the hit. A "tag up" can be made to second base, third base, or home plate. Of course, the possibility of making a given sacrifice fly out depends on the depth of the hit. It is not the same to throw out a runner at home on a hit at depth level 8 as at depth level 10. This play, which is made with fewer than 2 outs, must be announced by the batting player. The batter says "sacrifice" to a given base (or bases). The runners (if there are several) can all go on the "tag up" or only one of them, as the batter decides. But the fielder who will attempt the throw must choose where to try to make the out.

**Error:** When the ball lands on the error square on the field. Although its location varies, on most fields it is found between letters B and C. If it is an error, the batter advances one base, and so do the runners. It functions like a hit — if the batter used the Hit & Run card, the runner advances one extra base.

Note: Some fields have individual characteristics that do not change the way the game is played.

## III. PLAYERS

### BATTERS

Batters have 2 main characteristics, which will influence their batting ability.

* Strenght: which can be Light, Medium, or Heavy. This will determine which area to chose on the Septh Spinner.
* Prefered Hand: A player can be Right or Left Handed. This influences the Direction Spinner

### FIELDERS ON THE FIELD

In the **OUTFIELD** there are 3 players. The outfield goes from line 5 to 11; on some fields from line 6 to 11 (there are subtle differences between different table baseball fields). Generally it is beyond the infield dirt fan.

In the **INFIELD** there are 4 players who are distributed to cover the area as well as possible.

The **PITCHER** is placed at H1. However, if the batter is right-handed, the pitcher can shift to G1, and if the batter is left-handed, the pitcher can move to I1.

The catcher has no defensive representation in this table game.

For this reason, **EIGHT tokens (players)** are placed on the field, positioned by the fielding player according to each batter's characteristics and the game situation.

---

## IV. HOW IS AN OUT MADE ON THE FIELD?

Each field player (except the pitcher, who has a special characteristic) covers 4 squares around their position. Everything within that frame is an out. For example, if the left fielder is placed at C8, it means they catch any hit at C7, C8, C9; B7, B8, B9; D7, D8, D9. Anything beyond that is a hit, unless it is covered by another player. If I am at C8 and the hit lands at C10, I cannot reach it — it is a hit.

---

## V. HOW ARE HITS ACHIEVED ON THE FIELD?

**SINGLE** — When the ball's location is one step away. For example, if I am at C7 and the hit is at A10. In this case, the fielder crosses one point diagonally — this can only be done once.

**DOUBLE** — When the ball's location is two steps away. For example, if I am at C7 and the hit is at A11. In this case, I cross one point diagonally to A10 and another to A11. This is a double.

**TRIPLE** — When the ball's location is three steps away. For example, if I am at D7 and the hit is at A11. In this case, I cross one point diagonally to B10 (no more diagonals possible), a second step to A10, and finally another to A11. This is a triple.

**HOME RUN** — Generally, a home run occurs when the ball goes over the fence, which is almost always at number 12 (on some fields it is closer along the A and O lines). Also, when the hit's location is 4 steps away — in this case, it is an inside-the-park home run.
